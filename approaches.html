Value investing approaches

Value investing means paying less for a share than the business behind it is worth. Investors have found several ways to do that. Some buy assets cheaply, some buy great businesses at fair prices, and some buy growth or dependable dividends without overpaying. WorldValue runs six of these approaches over the same 14 markets. A company can qualify under more than one.

Every approach uses the same discipline. Keep price separate from value. Check earnings over several years, not one. Check the debt, including leases. Ask why the shares are cheap. Only call something a Pass if the case still holds when the numbers are 25% worse. Banks and insurers get their own tests. The result is always a case for or against the price, never an instruction to buy.

How to read the verdicts. Pass: the filings were read and the case survives a 25% error. Fail: it failed the rules, either on the filings or on the compiled figures before the accounts were opened. Filings not read: it meets the pre-screen on compiled data (Yahoo Finance annual figures with FT prices), but nobody has checked the accounts yet, so it is not a pass.

Deep value Benjamin Graham, Security Analysis (1934) and The Intelligent Investor (1949)

The idea: Buy a business for clearly less than its assets or its normal earnings, even if it is ordinary. Cheap first, quality second.

Graham, Warren Buffett’s teacher, bought ordinary companies when the price was well below what the assets or normal earnings were worth. Buffett later called this “cigar-butt” investing: a discarded cigar with one free puff left. It works because the market often overreacts to bad news, but the businesses are usually not ones to hold forever.

The rules used here

  • Trailing P/E under 12 on the FT figure (12 or more is treated as a full price and accounts are not opened).
  • Earnings checked over several years in the filings; one-offs, commodity spikes and revaluations removed.
  • Debt counted including leases; banks and insurers get their own capital test; landlords are tested against net tangible assets.
  • Pass only if the price is still below value after the value is cut by 25–30%.

Quality at a fair price Warren Buffett and Charlie Munger, Berkshire Hathaway

The idea: Pay a fair price for a wonderful business, one that earns high returns on capital year after year and can reinvest, rather than a poor business at a bargain price.

Munger pushed Buffett away from cigar butts towards great businesses. Buffett wrote in 1989: “It’s far better to buy a wonderful company at a fair price than a fair company at a wonderful price.” The turning point was See’s Candies, bought in 1972 for $25 million. It earned less than $5 million before tax then and needed little extra capital, yet by 2007 it earned $82 million a year. Loyal customers let it raise prices, which is a moat. The lesson is that high returns on capital, kept up for decades, compound value far more than a one-off discount.

Read more: Berkshire Hathaway 1989 letter ↗Berkshire Hathaway 2007 letter (See’s Candies) ↗

The rules used here

  • Return on invested capital (EBIT after 25% tax ÷ equity + debt − cash) averaging at least 15%, and never below 10%, over the years available. Financial companies use return on equity on the same bars.
  • Net debt including leases no more than 2× EBITDA (or net cash).
  • Free cash flow at least 80% of net income over the period (earnings turn into cash).
  • Stable margins: the worst operating margin at least two-thirds of the average.
  • FT trailing P/E above 0 and no more than 25.
  • Owner earnings = average free cash flow (after lease payments where read). Value = owner earnings ÷ 7.5% (a 10% required return less 2.5% growth) if revenue grew over the record, otherwise ÷ 10% (no growth). The pre-screen needs value ≥ 1.2× market value; a Pass needs ≥ 1.33×, so it survives a 25% error, plus a ten-year record read in the filings.
  • Holding companies are skipped. The pre-screen uses about four years of figures; the filings read checks up to ten.

Magic Formula Joel Greenblatt, The Little Book That Beats the Market (2005)

The idea: Rank companies on two things at once: how cheap they are (earnings yield) and how good they are (return on capital). Buy the ones that score best on both.

Greenblatt turned “good company at a cheap price” into two numbers anyone can rank: earnings yield (cheap) and return on capital (good). Buy the companies with the best combined rank, hold them for a year and repeat. He warned that the formula can lag for years at a time, which is partly why it keeps working.

Read more: Greenblatt’s Magic Formula site ↗

The rules used here

  • Earnings yield = EBIT ÷ enterprise value (market value + debt + minority interests − cash).
  • Return on capital = EBIT ÷ (net working capital + net fixed assets).
  • Financial companies, utilities, real estate and holding companies are excluded.
  • EBIT must be positive in each of the last 3–4 years, and the latest year no more than twice the earlier average (no one-off spikes).
  • Each company is ranked on both measures within its region; the two ranks are added and the top names in each region are shortlisted.
  • A Pass needs recurring EBIT confirmed in the filings, net debt no more than 2.5× EBITDA, and a normalised (multi-year average) earnings yield of at least 13.3%, so it is still 10% or more after a 25% cut.

Growth at a reasonable price Peter Lynch, One Up on Wall Street (1989)

The idea: A company growing its earnings steadily is worth buying if the price-earnings ratio is no higher than the growth rate (PEG of 1 or less).

Lynch ran Fidelity Magellan from 1977 to 1990. He liked steady growers that you can understand, bought at a P/E no higher than the growth rate. A PEG of 1 or less is his rule of thumb. Growth only counts if it is likely to continue, so the latest guidance matters as much as the past record.

The rules used here

  • Diluted EPS positive every year, with no down years in the figures available.
  • EPS growth between 8% and 30% a year (faster growth rarely lasts).
  • PEG = FT trailing P/E ÷ annual EPS growth (in %) of 1 or less.
  • Net debt no more than 2.5× EBITDA, or net cash. Banks and insurers are flagged rather than tested on debt.
  • A Pass needs five years of steady growth confirmed in the filings, a PEG still at or below 1 with growth cut by 25%, and the company’s latest guidance not pointing to lower earnings.

Dividend value Graham’s “defensive investor” and generations of income investors

The idea: A dependable, well-covered dividend that yields more than the market, bought at a sensible price, from a company that has not cut it in at least ten years.

Graham asked defensive investors for an unbroken dividend record of at least 20 years. A dividend paid out of real cash flow, kept up through bad years, is hard to fake and shows management discipline. A very high yield is often a warning that the market expects a cut, so cover and debt matter more than the yield itself.

The rules used here

  • Dividends summed by calendar year; at least 10 years in a row with no cut (a year more than 3% below the year before counts as a cut).
  • Trailing 12-month yield above the dividend yield of the MSCI index for that market (30 Sep 2026 factsheets).
  • FT trailing P/E above 0 and no more than 20. Special dividends excluded.
  • Non-financial companies: dividends paid no more than free cash flow, both in the latest year and over four years; net debt no more than 3× EBITDA.
  • Banks and insurers: dividends no more than 60% of net income, and a Pass also needs a capital-ratio read.
  • A Pass needs the record confirmed in the filings and free cash flow still covering the dividend after a 25% fall.

  • Pass: Home Depot (United States), Teleperformance (Continental Europe), ASTELLAS PHARMA INC. (Japan).

Net-nets Benjamin Graham, Security Analysis and The Intelligent Investor

The idea: Buy a company for less than two-thirds of its net current asset value, the cash, receivables and stock left after paying every liability, so the factories and brands come free.

Graham’s most extreme bargain: a company priced below two-thirds of its current assets minus all its liabilities, so the buildings, brands and future profits come free. Net-nets are usually small, troubled or unloved, and some fail. Graham bought them in baskets so that the winners outweighed the losers.

The rules used here

  • NCAV = current assets − total liabilities − preferred stock − minority interests.
  • Market value below two-thirds of NCAV. Names between two-thirds and one times NCAV are listed as near net-nets.
  • A Pass needs the formula confirmed from the latest balance sheet, and the price still below NCAV after NCAV is cut by 25%.
  • Data risk is high: inventory and receivables may be worth less than book, and the latest balance sheet may be stale. Every net-net carries that warning.
