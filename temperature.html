Reading the temperature

P/E against its own history

A market's P/E is the price of its shares divided by their earnings. Comparing it with the same index's own long-run average asks a simple question: are investors paying more or less than usual for each euro of profit? Each market is compared only with itself.

Why markets differ

Normal valuations differ because the businesses differ. The US index is heavy in technology companies that have grown fast and earn high returns, so it has usually traded on a higher P/E. Markets dominated by banks, miners and energy companies, such as the UK, Brazil or South Africa, usually trade lower. That is why one market's P/E is not compared with another's here.

CAPE

The cyclically adjusted P/E divides price by ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings, so one boom or bust year matters less. It is widely quoted for the US, where Robert Shiller's data go back to 1871.

What high valuations do and do not tell you

Over long periods, starting from a high valuation has tended to mean lower returns over the following ten years or so, and a low one higher returns. The relationship says little about the next year: expensive markets can get more expensive for a long time, and cheap ones cheaper.

No crash prediction

A Hot reading is not a forecast of a fall and a Cold reading is not a forecast of a rise. Earnings can catch up with prices, averages can shift as markets change, and the sources use different methods.

Method and sources

  • Temperature = current P/E ÷ the same index's long-run average − 1. Cold below −20%; Cool −20% to −5%; Fair −5% to +5%; Warm above +5% to +20%; Hot above +20%.
  • Like with like. The current P/E and the average always come from the same source and method: forward P/E against a forward average (J.P. Morgan, for the S&P 500, MSCI Europe ex-UK and FTSE All-Share), or trailing P/E against a trailing average (worldperatio.com, for the iShares MSCI country ETFs).
  • MSCI factsheet figures (P/E, forward P/E, P/BV, dividend yield) are shown for every market but are not compared with the averages, because MSCI does not publish a long-run average and other sources calculate P/E differently.
  • worldperatio.com is a third-party site that calculates P/E from ETF data and trims the top and bottom 10% of readings before averaging. Where an ETF has traded for less than 20 years, its 10-year average is used. Its current P/E can differ a lot from MSCI's own figure for the same index; where the gap is over 10%, the tile says so. These tiles are marked lower confidence.
  • MSCI factsheets are monthly and J.P. Morgan's Guide is quarterly; the page is checked weekly.
