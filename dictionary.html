Valuation (12)

CAPE (Shiller P/E)Valuation
Cyclically adjusted P/E: price divided by the average of the last ten years of inflation-adjusted earnings. Robert Shiller's measure smooths out booms and busts, so it says more about long-run value than about next year.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's inflation-adjusted earnings averaged €1.60 a share over the last ten years. At €20 a share its CAPE is €20 ÷ €1.60 = 12.5, above its trailing P/E of 10 because recent profit is higher than the ten-year average.

Earnings yieldValuation
EBIT divided by enterprise value: the operating profit you get for each euro paid for the whole business. Greenblatt's measure of cheapness. 10% is like paying 10 times operating profit.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's EBIT is €300m and its enterprise value €2,500m, so the earnings yield is €300m ÷ €2,500m = 12%.

Enterprise value (EV)Valuation
What it would cost to buy the whole business: market value of the shares plus debt and minority interests, minus cash. It lets you compare companies with different amounts of debt.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's market value is €2,000m, borrowings €600m and cash €100m, so EV is €2,000m + €600m − €100m = €2,500m.

Forward P/EValuation
Share price divided by the earnings analysts expect over the next twelve months. Usually lower than the trailing P/E when profits are expected to grow, and only as good as the forecasts.

Example (made-up Company X): Analysts expect Company X to earn €2.50 a share over the next twelve months. At €20 a share its forward P/E is €20 ÷ €2.50 = 8, below its trailing P/E of 10 because profit is expected to grow.

Margin of safetyValuation
The gap between price and estimated value. The test here is whether the price still looks cheap if the estimate is 20–30% too high.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's estimated value is €32 a share and the price is €20: €32 ÷ €20 = 1.6. If the estimate is 25% too high, the value is €32 × 0.75 = €24, still above the €20 price.

Market temperatureValuation
On this site, how far a market's current P/E is above or below its own long-run average, shown as Cold, Cool, Fair, Warm or Hot. A description of price against history, not a forecast.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's made-up home market trades on a P/E of 18 against its own 20-year average of 16: (18 − 16) ÷ 16 = 12.5%, which is in the Warm band (above 5% and up to 20%).

MedianValuation
The middle value when numbers are put in order. Less affected than the average by one extreme year, which is why long-run P/E records often quote both.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's P/E at the end of each of the last five years was 8, 9, 10, 12 and 26. The median is the middle value, 10, while the average is (8 + 9 + 10 + 12 + 26) ÷ 5 = 13, pulled up by one unusual year.

NAV discountValuation
How far the share price is below net asset value per share.

Example (made-up Company Y): Company Y's net asset value is €12 a share and its shares trade at €9. The discount is (€12 − €9) ÷ €12 = 25%.

P/E (trailing)Valuation
Share price divided by the last twelve months of earnings per share. Here, 12 or more counts as a full price.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's share costs €20 and it earned €2 a share over the last twelve months, so its P/E is €20 ÷ €2 = 10.

PEG ratioValuation
P/E divided by the yearly earnings growth rate in percent. Peter Lynch's rule of thumb: a PEG of 1 or less (for example a P/E of 15 with 15% growth) is a reasonable price for growth.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's P/E is 10 and its profit grows 10% a year, so its PEG is 10 ÷ 10 = 1.

Price-to-bookValuation
Market value divided by book value. Under 1 means the shares cost less than the accounts say they are worth.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's share costs €20 and book value is €10 a share, so price-to-book is €20 ÷ €10 = 2.

Standard deviationValuation
A measure of how far values usually stray from their average. About two-thirds of observations fall within one standard deviation either side of the average if the data are roughly bell-shaped.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's P/E has averaged 12 over 20 years with a standard deviation of 2, so in most years it was between 12 − 2 = 10 and 12 + 2 = 14.

Profit and earnings (14)

Adjusted earningsProfit and earnings
Profit as the company prefers to show it, with items it calls unusual taken out. Useful, but the company picks what to remove.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X adds back the €30m restructuring cost: €200m + €30m = €230m adjusted profit, or €230m ÷ 100m = €2.30 a share instead of €2.

Disposal gainProfit and earnings
Profit from selling a business or asset. It is real money but not repeatable earnings.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X sells a warehouse held in the accounts at €30m for €50m, a gain of €50m − €30m = €20m. It is real money, but it will not come again next year.

Earnings powerProfit and earnings
What a business can earn in an ordinary year, judged over several years rather than one good one.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X earned €150m, €250m, €180m, €220m and €200m over five years. Judged on the five years, its earning power is about €200m, not the €250m of its best year.

EBITProfit and earnings
Earnings before interest and tax: profit from operations before paying lenders and the taxman. Close to operating profit.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's operating profit before interest and tax is €2,000m − €1,700m = €300m. After €50m of interest, profit before tax is €300m − €50m = €250m.

EBITDAProfit and earnings
Operating profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. A rough measure of cash profit before investment.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's operating profit is €300m and depreciation and amortisation €100m, so EBITDA is €300m + €100m = €400m.

EPSProfit and earnings
Earnings per share: profit for shareholders divided by the number of shares.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's net profit is €200m and it has 100 million shares, so EPS is €200m ÷ 100m = €2.

Normalised earningsProfit and earnings
Earnings averaged over several years, so one very good or very bad year does not distort the price test.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X earned €150m, €250m, €180m, €220m and €200m over five years: (€150m + €250m + €180m + €220m + €200m) ÷ 5 = €200m a year.

One-off / exceptional itemsProfit and earnings
Gains or costs the company says will not repeat, such as selling a division or a big legal bill.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X closes a factory and books a €30m restructuring cost as exceptional. It reduces this year's profit but should not recur.

Operating profitProfit and earnings
Profit from the business itself, before interest and tax.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X has revenue of €2,000m and operating costs of €1,700m, so operating profit is €2,000m − €1,700m = €300m. The operating margin is €300m ÷ €2,000m = 15%.

Recurrent net incomeProfit and earnings
A landlord's profit from rent after costs and interest, leaving out property revaluations and sales.

Example (made-up Company Y): Company Y collects €100m of rent, spends €20m on costs and pays €30m of interest: €100m − €20m − €30m = €50m recurrent net income. A €40m rise in building valuations is left out.

Return on capital (Greenblatt)Profit and earnings
EBIT divided by net working capital plus net fixed assets: how much operating profit each euro of tangible capital produces. Greenblatt's measure of business quality.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's EBIT is €300m, working capital €300m and net fixed assets €900m: €300m ÷ (€300m + €900m) = 25%.

ROEProfit and earnings
Return on equity: net profit divided by shareholders' equity. Used instead of ROIC for banks and insurers. Watch for high ROE that comes only from heavy debt or from buybacks that shrink equity.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's net profit is €200m and shareholders' equity €1,000m, so ROE is €200m ÷ €1,000m = 20%.

ROICProfit and earnings
Return on invested capital: operating profit after tax divided by the money tied up in the business (equity plus debt minus cash). Here, EBIT × 0.75 ÷ (equity + debt − cash). A business that earns 15% or more year after year usually has something competitors cannot copy.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's EBIT is €300m, so after the standard 25% tax used here it is €300m × 0.75 = €225m. Invested capital is €1,000m + €600m − €100m = €1,500m, so ROIC is €225m ÷ €1,500m = 15%.

Statutory earningsProfit and earnings
Profit as reported under the accounting rules, including one-off gains and losses.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X reports a net profit of €200m under the accounting rules. That figure includes a €30m one-off restructuring cost.

Balance sheet and debt (11)

Book valueBalance sheet and debt
What the accounts say shareholders own: assets minus liabilities.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's assets are €2,100m and its liabilities €1,100m, so book value is €2,100m − €1,100m = €1,000m, or €1,000m ÷ 100m = €10 a share.

EPRA NTABalance sheet and debt
Net tangible assets per share on the property industry's standard basis: roughly what a landlord's shares are worth if the valuations are right.

Example (made-up Company Y): Company Y's buildings are worth €1,000m and its debt is €400m, leaving €1,000m − €400m = €600m of net tangible assets. With 50 million shares that is €600m ÷ 50m = €12 a share.

Goodwill / intangiblesBalance sheet and debt
Assets you cannot touch, such as brands or the premium paid for an acquisition. They can lose value quickly.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X paid €500m for a business whose net assets were €200m. The extra €500m − €200m = €300m is recorded as goodwill.

ImpairmentBalance sheet and debt
A write-down when an asset is judged to be worth less than its book value. It cuts reported profit.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X decides the business it bought is worth less and writes goodwill down from €300m to €250m. The €300m − €250m = €50m impairment cuts reported profit, though no cash leaves the company.

Interest coverBalance sheet and debt
Operating profit divided by interest paid. Shows how easily the company can pay its lenders.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's operating profit is €300m and it pays €50m of interest, so interest cover is €300m ÷ €50m = 6.

Leases in net debtBalance sheet and debt
Long rent contracts count as a kind of borrowing, so they are added to debt for shops, airlines, factories and landlords.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X also has €200m of long shop leases. Net debt including leases is €500m + €200m = €700m.

Loan-to-value (LTV)Balance sheet and debt
A landlord's debt as a share of what its buildings are valued at. Lower is safer.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up landlord Company Y owns buildings valued at €1,000m and has debt of €400m, so LTV is €400m ÷ €1,000m = 40%.

NCAVBalance sheet and debt
Net current asset value: current assets (cash, receivables, stock) minus all liabilities, including long-term debt. Graham's very conservative estimate of what shareholders might get in a liquidation.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's current assets (cash, money owed by customers and stock) are €100m + €250m + €200m = €550m, and all its liabilities €1,100m. NCAV is €550m − €1,100m = −€550m, so it is nowhere near a net-net.

Net debtBalance sheet and debt
Borrowings minus cash. What the company would still owe if it used all its cash to repay debt.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X has borrowings of €600m and cash of €100m, so net debt is €600m − €100m = €500m.

Net debt/EBITDABalance sheet and debt
How many years of EBITDA it would take to pay off net debt. Above about 3 is heavy for most businesses.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's net debt is €500m and EBITDA €400m: €500m ÷ €400m = 1.25. Including leases it is €700m ÷ €400m = 1.75, still under 2.

RevaluationBalance sheet and debt
A change in the book value of assets such as buildings to match a new valuation. It moves profit without any cash.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's office building, held at €100m, is revalued at €120m. Book value rises by €120m − €100m = €20m without any cash coming in.

Cash flow (3)

Free cash flowCash flow
Cash from operations minus spending on equipment and buildings. What is left for debt, dividends and buybacks.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's cash from operations is €320m and it spends €80m on equipment and buildings, so free cash flow is €320m − €80m = €240m.

Owner earningsCash flow
Buffett's term for the cash a business could pay its owners each year while staying as strong: roughly profit plus depreciation, minus the investment needed to maintain it. Here, average free cash flow, after lease payments where the filings were read.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's free cash flow was €200m, €220m, €240m, €260m and €280m over five years: (€200m + €220m + €240m + €260m + €280m) ÷ 5 = €240m. Because revenue grew, it is valued at 7.5%: €240m ÷ 7.5% = €3,200m, or €32 a share.

Working capitalCash flow
Short-term assets such as stock and money owed by customers, minus short-term bills. Ties up cash as a business grows.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X holds €200m of stock and is owed €250m by customers, and owes suppliers €150m: €200m + €250m − €150m = €300m of working capital.

Dividends and buybacks (4)

BuybackDividends and buybacks
The company buying its own shares, which spreads future profit over fewer shares.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X spends €400m buying back shares at €20, which is €400m ÷ €20 = 20m shares. The same €200m profit spread over 100m − 20m = 80m shares is €200m ÷ 80m = €2.50 a share.

Dividend coverDividends and buybacks
Free cash flow (or earnings) divided by dividends paid. Above 1 means the dividend is paid from cash the business generated; here it must stay above 1 after a 25% fall.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's free cash flow is €240m and it pays €100m of dividends: €240m ÷ €100m = 2.4. After a 25% fall in cash flow it is €240m × 0.75 ÷ €100m = 1.8, still above 1.

Dividend yieldDividends and buybacks
Yearly dividend per share divided by the share price.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X pays a dividend of €1 a share and its share costs €20, so the yield is €1 ÷ €20 = 5%.

Payout ratioDividends and buybacks
Dividends as a share of earnings (or of free cash flow). Below about 60–70% leaves room for a bad year; above 100% means the dividend is not being earned.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X pays a €1 dividend out of €2 of earnings a share, so its payout ratio is €1 ÷ €2 = 50%.

Approaches and verdicts (12)

Closest missApproaches and verdicts
A name that came near passing but failed on one point.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up Company Y passed every test except value, where it came out at 1.25 times market value against the 1.33 needed.

Compiled figuresApproaches and verdicts
Accounts numbers taken from a data provider rather than read from the company's own report. Good for screening, not enough for a pass.

Example (made-up Company X): A data provider shows Company X's net profit as €200m. Reading the annual report shows that €30m of costs were one-offs, which the compiled figure does not explain.

Deep valueApproaches and verdicts
Graham's approach: buy ordinary businesses for clearly less than their assets or normal earnings. Also called cigar-butt investing: one last free puff.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up Company Y has book value of €20 a share but trades at €12, because profits have slumped. A deep-value investor buys it for less than its assets.

Dividend valueApproaches and verdicts
Buying dependable dividend payers with a long record of no cuts, a yield above the market's and a dividend well covered by free cash flow.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X yields 5%, has not cut its €1 dividend in ten years and covers it 2.4 times with free cash flow.

Full priceApproaches and verdicts
A multiple of 12 or more, so the accounts were not opened.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up Company Y's share costs €30 and it earns €2 a share, so its P/E is €30 ÷ €2 = 15. That is 12 or more, so its accounts were not opened.

GARPApproaches and verdicts
Growth at a reasonable price: Peter Lynch's approach of buying steady growers whose P/E is no higher than their growth rate.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's P/E is 10 and its profit grows 10% a year. Since the P/E is no higher than the growth rate, it fits GARP.

Magic FormulaApproaches and verdicts
Joel Greenblatt's ranking: buy the companies with the best combined rank on earnings yield (cheap) and return on capital (good). Financials and utilities excluded.

Example (made-up Company X): Among 100 made-up companies, Company X ranks 5th on earnings yield (12%) and 10th on return on capital (25%). Its combined score is 5 + 10 = 15; the lowest scores are bought.

MoatApproaches and verdicts
Buffett's word for a lasting competitive advantage, such as a brand, low costs, network or switching costs, that lets a business keep earning high returns. See's Candies, with its loyal customers and pricing power, is the classic example.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's customers keep years of records in its software, and moving them would cost time and money. It can raise prices a little each year without losing many customers.

Net-netApproaches and verdicts
A share priced below two-thirds of its NCAV, so you pay nothing for factories, brands or future profits. Graham's favourite bargain; often troubled companies, so buy many and expect some to fail.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up Company Y has current assets of €400m and total liabilities of €100m, so NCAV is €400m − €100m = €300m. Two-thirds of that is €300m × 2 ÷ 3 = €200m, and its market value is €150m, so it is a net-net.

PassApproaches and verdicts
A name where both the accounts and the price hold up after the margin-of-safety test. A case about the price, not a buy.

Example (made-up Company X): After reading Company X's annual reports, its value of €32 a share still beats its €20 price after a 25% haircut (€32 × 0.75 = €24), so it is a pass: a case about the price, not a buy.

Quality at a fair priceApproaches and verdicts
Buffett and Munger's approach: a wonderful business at a fair price beats a fair business at a wonderful price. High, steady returns on capital, little debt, cash earnings, and a price below a conservative value.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X earns 15% on capital, its net debt including leases is 1.75 times EBITDA, free cash flow beats net profit (€240m against €200m), and its estimated value of €32 is 1.6 times its €20 price.

ShortlistApproaches and verdicts
Names that survived the test on compiled figures but whose filings were not read, so they are not passes.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X passes the test on compiled figures, but its annual report has not been read yet, so it sits on the shortlist, not the pass list.

Risks (5)

Commodity price exposureRisks
When profit depends on prices of oil, coal, metals or crops that the company does not control.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up oil producer Company Y sells 10 million barrels a year. If the oil price rises by €10 a barrel, its revenue rises by 10m × €10 = €100m, though it did nothing differently.

Currency riskRisks
A share priced in another currency can lose value in euros even if the price does not move.

Example (made-up Company X): A made-up US share costs $120. At $1.20 to the euro that is $120 ÷ 1.20 = €100. If the rate moves to $1.25, the same $120 is worth $120 ÷ 1.25 = €96.

CyclicalRisks
A business whose profit rises and falls with the economy, such as cars, building or steel.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X earns €200m in a normal year but €80m in a recession, a fall of (€200m − €80m) ÷ €200m = 60%.

Rate cycleRisks
The rise and fall of interest rates. Bank profits often look best when rates are high.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up bank Company Y lends €1,000m and earns 3% more than it pays depositors: 3% × €1,000m = €30m. If rates fall and that gap shrinks to 2%, it earns 2% × €1,000m = €20m.

State control / minority shareholder riskRisks
When a government controls a company, it can put policy ahead of other shareholders, for example by capping prices or holding back dividends.

Example (made-up Company Y): A government owns 60% of made-up energy firm Company Y and orders it to freeze prices before an election. Profit falls, and the other 40% of shareholders cannot outvote the state.

Funds and tax (10)

AccumulatingFunds and tax
A fund share class that reinvests dividends inside the fund instead of paying them out. Often shown as Acc.

Example (made-up Fund X): A made-up Fund X Acc receives €50 in dividends on your holding and buys more shares with it inside the fund. You receive no cash, but each unit is worth more.

Deemed disposalFunds and tax
An Irish tax rule for most EU-domiciled funds and ETFs: every 8 years you are taxed as if you had sold and bought back, even if you did not sell. Tax paid is credited when you finally sell.

Example (made-up Fund X): You put €10,000 into a made-up Fund X ETF in 2026 and it is worth €15,000 in 2034. After 8 years you are taxed as if you sold: €15,000 − €10,000 = €5,000 gain, and 38% × €5,000 = €1,900 tax. The €1,900 is credited against the tax due when you finally sell.

DistributingFunds and tax
A fund share class that pays dividends out to investors, usually quarterly or twice a year. Often shown as Dist.

Example (made-up Fund X): You hold 100 units of a made-up Fund X Dist, which pays €0.50 a unit twice a year: 100 × €0.50 × 2 = €100 a year paid to you.

ETFFunds and tax
Exchange-traded fund: a fund whose shares trade on a stock exchange during the day like a company's shares. Most ETFs are index funds.

Example (made-up Fund X): You buy 10 units of a made-up Fund X ETF at €50 at 11am on the exchange, paying 10 × €50 = €500. Its price moves during the day like a share.

Exit taxFunds and tax
In Ireland, the tax on gains and income from Irish and equivalent EU/EEA/OECD funds, charged instead of capital gains tax. See Revenue.ie for the current rate.

Example (made-up Fund X): You buy units in a made-up Fund X for €10,000 and sell them for €12,000. The gain is €12,000 − €10,000 = €2,000, and exit tax at 38% is 38% × €2,000 = €760.

Index fundFunds and tax
A fund that simply holds the shares in a market index (such as the S&P 500) in roughly the same proportions, instead of a manager picking stocks. Costs are usually low.

Example (made-up Company X): A made-up index has Company X at 2% of its total value. An index fund tracking it puts 2% of its money into Company X: €2 of every €100 invested.

Investment trustFunds and tax
A listed company whose business is holding other investments. Left out of this screen.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up Company Y Trust is a listed company whose only business is holding shares in 50 other companies, so this screen leaves it out.

PRIIPs KIDFunds and tax
Key Information Document: a short, standard EU summary of a fund's costs, risks and scenarios that must be provided before a retail investor buys.

Example (made-up Fund X): Before you buy a made-up Fund X, the platform shows its three-page KID: yearly costs, a risk score from 1 to 7 and what you might get back in a bad, middling or good market.

TERFunds and tax
Total expense ratio, also called the ongoing charge: the yearly cost of running a fund, taken from the fund's assets. 0.20% means about 2 euro a year for every 1,000 euro invested.

Example (made-up Fund X): A made-up Fund X has a TER of 0.20%. On €10,000 invested, the yearly cost is 0.20% × €10,000 = €20.

UCITSFunds and tax
The EU rulebook for investment funds sold to the public (Undertakings for Collective Investment in Transferable Securities). It sets limits on risk, diversification and disclosure.

Example (made-up Fund X): A made-up Fund X sold to the public in Ireland under UCITS rules generally cannot put more than 10% of its money into Company X, however much the manager likes it.

Market basics (12)

A sharesMarket basics
Shares of Chinese companies listed in Shanghai or Shenzhen. Left out here because Irish retail investors cannot readily buy them.

Example (made-up Company Y): The same made-up Company Y also lists shares in Shanghai, priced in yuan. Those are its A shares, which this screen leaves out.

ADRMarket basics
American Depositary Receipt: a US-traded certificate that stands for shares of a foreign company.

Example (made-up Company Y): One ADR of made-up Company Y stands for 2 of its shares. With each share worth €10, an ADR is worth about 2 × €10 = €20 before fees.

Cayman / Bermuda incorporationMarket basics
Many Hong Kong-listed companies are legally registered offshore. Shareholder rights follow that country's law.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up Company Y runs factories in mainland China but is registered in the Cayman Islands and listed in Hong Kong. Shareholders' rights follow Cayman law.

Constituent listMarket basics
The dated list of companies in an index, used as the starting universe.

Example (made-up Company X): A made-up index list dated 1 October 2026 names 100 companies, including Company X. The screen starts from those 100.

H sharesMarket basics
Shares of mainland Chinese companies listed in Hong Kong and traded in Hong Kong dollars.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up Company Y is based in Beijing and lists some of its shares in Hong Kong, priced in Hong Kong dollars. Those are its H shares.

ISINMarket basics
A 12-character code that identifies one security worldwide.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's shares might carry a made-up ISIN such as IE000EXAMPL1: two letters for the country, nine characters for the security and a check digit.

Market valueMarket basics
Share price times the number of shares: what the market charges for the whole company.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X has 100 million shares at €20, so its market value is 100m × €20 = €2,000m.

Minor unitsMarket basics
Some exchanges quote prices in cents (Johannesburg) or agorot (Tel Aviv). The dashboard shows rand and shekels.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up Company Y on the Johannesburg exchange is quoted at 2,000 cents. That is 2,000 ÷ 100 = R20 a share.

Primary listingMarket basics
The main exchange where a company is listed and regulated.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X's shares are listed and regulated mainly on the Dublin stock exchange, which is its primary listing.

REITMarket basics
A real estate investment trust: a landlord that pays out most of its rent as dividends in return for tax relief.

Example (made-up Company Y): Made-up landlord Company Y REIT earns €50m of recurrent net income and pays out €45m as dividends, which is €45m ÷ €50m = 90%.

Secondary listingMarket basics
An extra listing on another exchange. This screen judges each company only on its primary market.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X also lists its shares in London so more investors can buy them. This screen still judges it only on its Dublin market.

TickerMarket basics
The short code a share trades under, here with the FT exchange suffix such as :TYO or :HKG.

Example (made-up Company X): Company X might trade under a made-up ticker such as CMPYX, which is shorter to type than its full name.
