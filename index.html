Finance dictionary (50 terms)

P/E (trailing)
Share price divided by the last twelve months of earnings per share. Here, 12 or more counts as a full price.
Statutory earnings
Profit as reported under the accounting rules, including one-off gains and losses.
Adjusted earnings
Profit as the company prefers to show it, with items it calls unusual taken out. Useful, but the company picks what to remove.
EPS
Earnings per share: profit for shareholders divided by the number of shares.
Book value
What the accounts say shareholders own: assets minus liabilities.
Price-to-book
Market value divided by book value. Under 1 means the shares cost less than the accounts say they are worth.
Net debt
Borrowings minus cash. What the company would still owe if it used all its cash to repay debt.
Leases in net debt
Long rent contracts count as a kind of borrowing, so they are added to debt for shops, airlines, factories and landlords.
EBITDA
Operating profit before interest, tax, depreciation and amortisation. A rough measure of cash profit before investment.
Net debt/EBITDA
How many years of EBITDA it would take to pay off net debt. Above about 3 is heavy for most businesses.
Interest cover
Operating profit divided by interest paid. Shows how easily the company can pay its lenders.
Operating profit
Profit from the business itself, before interest and tax.
Loan-to-value (LTV)
A landlord's debt as a share of what its buildings are valued at. Lower is safer.
EPRA NTA
Net tangible assets per share on the property industry's standard basis: roughly what a landlord's shares are worth if the valuations are right.
Recurrent net income
A landlord's profit from rent after costs and interest, leaving out property revaluations and sales.
NAV discount
How far the share price is below net asset value per share.
Margin of safety
The gap between price and estimated value. The test here is whether the price still looks cheap if the estimate is 20–30% too high.
Earnings power
What a business can earn in an ordinary year, judged over several years rather than one good one.
Dividend yield
Yearly dividend per share divided by the share price.
Free cash flow
Cash from operations minus spending on equipment and buildings. What is left for debt, dividends and buybacks.
Working capital
Short-term assets such as stock and money owed by customers, minus short-term bills. Ties up cash as a business grows.
Goodwill / intangibles
Assets you cannot touch, such as brands or the premium paid for an acquisition. They can lose value quickly.
Impairment
A write-down when an asset is judged to be worth less than its book value. It cuts reported profit.
Revaluation
A change in the book value of assets such as buildings to match a new valuation. It moves profit without any cash.
One-off / exceptional items
Gains or costs the company says will not repeat, such as selling a division or a big legal bill.
Disposal gain
Profit from selling a business or asset. It is real money but not repeatable earnings.
Buyback
The company buying its own shares, which spreads future profit over fewer shares.
Market value
Share price times the number of shares: what the market charges for the whole company.
Primary listing
The main exchange where a company is listed and regulated.
Secondary listing
An extra listing on another exchange. This screen judges each company only on its primary market.
ISIN
A 12-character code that identifies one security worldwide.
Ticker
The short code a share trades under, here with the FT exchange suffix such as :TYO or :HKG.
Investment trust
A listed company whose business is holding other investments. Left out of this screen.
REIT
A real estate investment trust: a landlord that pays out most of its rent as dividends in return for tax relief.
Cyclical
A business whose profit rises and falls with the economy, such as cars, building or steel.
Commodity price exposure
When profit depends on prices of oil, coal, metals or crops that the company does not control.
Rate cycle
The rise and fall of interest rates. Bank profits often look best when rates are high.
State control / minority shareholder risk
When a government controls a company, it can put policy ahead of other shareholders, for example by capping prices or holding back dividends.
H shares
Shares of mainland Chinese companies listed in Hong Kong and traded in Hong Kong dollars.
A shares
Shares of Chinese companies listed in Shanghai or Shenzhen. Left out here because Irish retail investors cannot readily buy them.
Cayman / Bermuda incorporation
Many Hong Kong-listed companies are legally registered offshore. Shareholder rights follow that country's law.
ADR
American Depositary Receipt: a US-traded certificate that stands for shares of a foreign company.
Currency risk
A share priced in another currency can lose value in euros even if the price does not move.
Minor units
Some exchanges quote prices in cents (Johannesburg) or agorot (Tel Aviv). The dashboard shows rand and shekels.
Compiled figures
Accounts numbers taken from a data provider rather than read from the company's own report. Good for screening, not enough for a pass.
Shortlist
Names that survived the test on compiled figures but whose filings were not read, so they are not passes.
Pass
A name where both the accounts and the price hold up after the margin-of-safety test. A case about the price, not a buy.
Closest miss
A name that came near passing but failed on one point.
Full price
A multiple of 12 or more, so the accounts were not opened.
Constituent list
The dated list of companies in an index, used as the starting universe.

  • Investopedia: Book value – what book value is and how it is worked out
  • Investopedia: Net debt – debt minus cash, and why it matters
  • EPRA reporting guidelines – the property industry's rules behind NTA and recurrent earnings

Valuation

  • Aswath Damodaran, NYU Stern – free valuation notes, spreadsheets and data from a valuation professor
  • Damodaran: free online valuation class – recorded lectures on valuing companies
  • Damodaran: current data – multiples and margins by industry and region
  • Investopedia: P/E ratio – what the price/earnings multiple tells you
  • Investopedia: Price-to-book – comparing price with book value

Margin of safety

  • Investopedia: Margin of safety – the idea behind the screen's 20–30% test
  • Investopedia: Value investing – the approach in short
  • Howard Marks memos, Oaktree – free memos on risk, cycles and price

Books

  • The Intelligent Investor – Benjamin Graham – the founding book for private investors: price versus value and keeping a cushion
  • Security Analysis – Graham & Dodd – the detailed textbook on reading companies and bonds behind value investing
  • The Little Book of Value Investing – Christopher Browne – a short, easy start on buying good businesses cheaply
  • The Most Important Thing – Howard Marks – thinking about risk, cycles and what the price already assumes
  • Margin of Safety – Seth Klarman – why avoiding losses comes first; out of print, so no publisher page
  • The Essays of Warren Buffett – ed. Lawrence Cunningham – Buffett's shareholder letters arranged by theme
  • Common Stocks and Uncommon Profits – Philip Fisher – how to judge business quality and management, not just price
  • You Can Be a Stock Market Genius – Joel Greenblatt – special situations such as spin-offs and restructurings
  • The Little Book That Still Beats the Market – Joel Greenblatt – a simple rule for buying good companies at low prices
  • Financial Shenanigans – Howard Schilit – spotting accounts that flatter profits or hide problems
  • The Little Book of Valuation – Aswath Damodaran – a plain guide to putting a value on a company
  • Poor Charlie's Almanack – Charles Munger – Munger's talks on clear thinking and avoiding mistakes
  • One Up on Wall Street – Peter Lynch – using what you see day to day to find shares worth researching
  • Value Investing: From Graham to Buffett and Beyond – Bruce Greenwald – the Columbia method: asset value, earnings power and moats

  • Central Bank of Ireland: consumer explainers – the regulator's guides, including checking a firm is authorised
